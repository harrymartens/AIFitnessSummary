# Sleep Science: Recovery, Performance, and Interpretation

## Sleep Architecture

Human sleep cycles through approximately 90-minute ultradian cycles, each comprising distinct stages with different physiological roles:

- **NREM Stage 1 (N1):** Light sleep, transition from wakefulness. Minutes in duration; easily disturbed.
- **NREM Stage 2 (N2):** Deeper sleep characterised by sleep spindles and K-complexes. Memory consolidation begins. Comprises roughly 50% of total sleep.
- **NREM Stage 3 (N3 — Slow Wave Sleep / Deep Sleep):** The most physically restorative stage. Delta brain waves dominate. Growth hormone secretion, tissue repair, and immune function peak here. Predominantly occurs in the first half of the night.
- **REM Sleep:** Characterised by rapid eye movement, motor atonia, and vivid dreaming. Critical for procedural memory, emotional regulation, and skill consolidation. Predominantly occurs in the second half of the night, with REM periods lengthening in later cycles.

A full night of quality sleep contains 4–6 complete cycles. Disrupting the second half of sleep disproportionately reduces REM; disrupting the first half cuts into deep sleep.

## Sleep and Muscle Protein Synthesis

Slow wave sleep is the primary window for anabolic hormone release. Growth hormone (GH) secretion in trained adults occurs in large pulses during N3, with 70–80% of daily GH output released during sleep. GH drives muscle protein synthesis (MPS), lipolysis, and tissue repair. Testosterone — the other primary anabolic hormone — is also synthesised during sleep, with levels peaking in early morning REM.

Consequently, sleep restriction directly impairs the hormonal environment for muscle growth and recovery. Even partial sleep deprivation (5–6 hrs vs 8 hrs) over one week reduces testosterone by 10–15% (Leproult & Van Cauter, 2011).

## Sleep Debt and Performance Degradation

Matthew Walker's research (summarised in *Why We Sleep*, 2017) and supporting laboratory studies document consistent performance decrements with sleep restriction:

- **Strength:** Maximal strength and muscular endurance decline measurably after 3–5 nights at <7 hours.
- **Reaction time:** 17 hours of wakefulness produces cognitive impairment equivalent to 0.05% blood alcohol. Sleep-deprived athletes exhibit 20–30% slower reaction times.
- **Mood and motivation:** Sleep loss elevates amygdala reactivity by ~60%, increasing negative emotional responses and reducing perceived effort tolerance.
- **Injury risk:** Athletes averaging <8 hours of sleep have 1.7x greater injury incidence (Milewski et al., 2014).

Performance deficits accumulate cumulatively and are subjectively underestimated — individuals rarely feel as impaired as they actually are.

## Sleep Quality Markers (Garmin / Wearable Context)

Wearable sleep tracking uses actigraphy and heart rate variability to estimate sleep stages. While not as accurate as polysomnography, trends are clinically meaningful:

- **Total sleep:** Target 7–9 hours for most adults (National Sleep Foundation). Athletes may benefit from 8–10 hours during high-volume training blocks.
- **Deep sleep (N3):** Healthy adults average 13–23% of total sleep (~1–2 hours on 8 hours total). Values below 15% with total sleep under 7.5 hours suggest inadequate physical recovery.
- **REM sleep:** Typically 20–25% of total sleep (~90–120 min). Consistently low REM (<15%) may reflect alcohol consumption, late-night high-intensity exercise, or circadian disruption.
- **Awake time:** Brief awakenings are normal; >45 min/night of recorded awake time indicates fragmented sleep.

## Recovery Strategies for Sleep Quality

Evidence-ranked interventions for improving sleep quality:

1. **Consistent wake time:** The single most impactful behaviour. Anchors the circadian rhythm regardless of bedtime variation.
2. **Cool sleeping environment (18–20°C / 64–68°F):** Core body temperature must drop 1–2°C to initiate and maintain sleep. A cool room accelerates this.
3. **Darkness:** Even dim light (10 lux) suppresses melatonin by 50%. Blackout curtains or a sleep mask are effective and evidence-supported.
4. **Screen avoidance (1 hr pre-bed):** Blue-wavelength light delays melatonin onset by 90 minutes. Blue-light glasses reduce but do not eliminate this effect.
5. **Alcohol avoidance:** Alcohol is sedating but fragments sleep architecture — it dramatically suppresses REM in the second half of the night.
6. **Caffeine cutoff:** Caffeine's half-life is 5–7 hours. A 200 mg dose at 3pm still has 100 mg active at 9pm.

## Napping

A 20-minute "power nap" — timed to avoid N3 onset (which causes sleep inertia) — restores alertness and improves cognitive performance for 2–3 hours without significantly disrupting nighttime sleep if taken before 3pm. Naps over 30 minutes risk sleep inertia and may reduce sleep pressure (adenosine clearance) for the subsequent night. For athletes with significant sleep debt, longer recovery naps (60–90 min, full cycle) can be strategic.

## Interpreting Low Deep Sleep and Low REM in Training Context

- **Low deep sleep after high-volume training:** May reflect genuine fatigue and increased slow wave rebound (the body prioritising physical recovery). Paradoxically, very high training load can temporarily reduce measured deep sleep percentages as absolute duration may compress.
- **Consistently low deep sleep (<1 hour):** Flag for recovery insufficiency; correlates with elevated resting HR and suppressed HRV the following morning.
- **Low REM following intense training days:** Normal; the nervous system temporarily prioritises N3 over REM. Persistent REM suppression across easy days warrants investigation (stress, alcohol, inconsistent schedule).
- **Wearable accuracy note:** Stage classifications from consumer wearables can be up to 30% inaccurate for individual nights. Focus on multi-night trends rather than single-night readings.
