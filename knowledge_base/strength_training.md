# Strength Training: Evidence-Based Principles

## Progressive Overload

Progressive overload is the foundational principle of all strength adaptation, first systematised by Thomas DeLorme (1945) and later formalised in modern sport science. The principle holds that the training stimulus must consistently exceed what the body is accustomed to in order to drive further adaptation. Zatsiorsky's classic framework identifies three mechanisms: the maximal effort method (near-maximal loads), the dynamic effort method (submaximal loads moved explosively), and the repeated effort method (moderate loads to failure). Schoenfeld's meta-analyses confirm that all three can produce hypertrophy, but progressive overload — whether through added load, reps, sets, or reduced rest — is the non-negotiable driver.

In practice, progressive overload means tracking performance across sessions and systematically increasing challenge. Even small increments (0.5–2.5 kg per session for isolation exercises, 2.5–5 kg for compound lifts) compound meaningfully over months.

## Volume Landmarks (Renaissance Periodization / Israetel Framework)

Mike Israetel's volume landmark model provides a practical framework for programming:

- **Minimum Effective Volume (MEV):** The least weekly volume needed to make progress. Typically 4–8 working sets per muscle group per week for trained individuals. Below this, maintenance or detraining occurs.
- **Maximum Adaptive Volume (MAV):** The volume range producing optimal gains, usually 12–20 sets per muscle group per week, though highly individual. This is the target zone for most training blocks.
- **Maximum Recoverable Volume (MRV):** The ceiling above which accumulated fatigue prevents recovery and adaptation declines. Exceeding MRV for extended periods risks overtraining. MRV rises with training age and varies by muscle group (legs and back tolerate more than, e.g., triceps).

Weekly volume should sit between MEV and MAV during accumulation phases, approaching MRV near the end of a block before a deload.

## Rep Ranges and Training Goals

Research by Schoenfeld (2017) and subsequent meta-analyses supports the following practical rep-range guidelines:

- **1–5 reps (85–100% 1RM):** Prioritises maximal strength via neural adaptations (motor unit recruitment, rate coding). Best for compound lifts (squat, deadlift, bench, overhead press).
- **6–12 reps (67–85% 1RM):** The "hypertrophy range" — optimises mechanical tension and metabolic stress. Most accessible range for muscle growth in both novice and advanced lifters.
- **15+ reps (<67% 1RM):** Primarily muscular endurance; can still drive hypertrophy when taken close to failure, but typically less time-efficient for size and strength.

For most athletes pursuing both strength and size, blending rep ranges across a programme is superior to any single range.

## Periodisation Types

- **Linear periodisation:** Loads increase progressively each week; volume decreases. Effective for beginners. Simple to implement.
- **Daily Undulating Periodisation (DUP):** Rep ranges rotate within the same week (e.g., heavy Monday, moderate Wednesday, high-rep Friday). Better for intermediate-to-advanced lifters who need varied stimuli and have adapted to linear models.
- **Block periodisation:** Multi-week phases emphasise a single quality (accumulation → intensification → realisation). Used by competitive strength athletes for competition peaking.

## Deload Protocols

Deloads allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate, revealing the underlying fitness gains. Signs that a deload is due include stalled performance, elevated resting HR, poor sleep, persistent soreness, and low motivation. Typical deload strategies:

- **Volume deload (most common):** Reduce total sets by 40–60%; maintain load and exercise selection. Duration: 1 week.
- **Intensity deload:** Keep volume similar but drop to ~60–70% of normal loads.
- **Frequency deload:** Drop training days from 4–5 to 2–3; maintain normal sets/loads on training days.

Planned deloads every 4–8 weeks (depending on training age and stress load) prevent MRV violation and accumulation of chronic fatigue.

## Compound vs Isolation Exercise Prioritisation

Compound multi-joint movements (squat, deadlift, bench press, row, overhead press) produce the greatest systemic hormonal response, recruit multiple muscle groups simultaneously, and deliver the highest return on training time. They should anchor every session.

Isolation exercises (curls, lateral raises, leg extensions) allow targeted volume for lagging muscles and are best programmed after compound work, when neural demand is lower. A well-structured session typically follows: compound (heavy) → compound (moderate) → isolation.

## Rest Periods

- **Strength (1–5 rep range):** 3–5 minutes between sets to allow near-complete phosphocreatine resynthesis and maintain force output across sets.
- **Hypertrophy (6–12 reps):** 90–120 seconds is the traditional recommendation; however, Schoenfeld (2016) demonstrated that 3-minute rests produce superior hypertrophy compared to 1-minute rests, likely due to greater total volume capacity. Practical target: 2–3 minutes.
- **Endurance (15+ reps):** 30–60 seconds; metabolic conditioning effect is partly the goal.

## Training Frequency

Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) peaks 24–36 hours post-session and returns to baseline by ~48–72 hours. Training each muscle group twice per week is therefore superior to once-per-week (Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger, 2016 meta-analysis). Higher frequencies (3x/week) may benefit advanced trainees managing volume distribution, but the marginal gain diminishes. Two sessions per muscle group per week is the minimum evidence-based standard for hypertrophy.
