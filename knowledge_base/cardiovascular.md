# Cardiovascular Fitness: Metrics, Zones, and Adaptations

## VO2 Max: The Gold Standard Aerobic Metric

VO2 max (maximal oxygen uptake) represents the highest rate at which the body can consume oxygen during maximal exercise. It is expressed in ml/kg/min (relative to body weight) and is the most powerful single predictor of both aerobic performance and long-term cardiovascular health. Higher VO2 max correlates with lower all-cause mortality, even after controlling for other risk factors (Kodama et al., 2009 meta-analysis; Ross et al., 2016 landmark study).

**ACSM Population Norms (ml/kg/min, approximate):**

| Age | Female (Good) | Male (Good) |
|-----|--------------|-------------|
| 20–29 | 43–48 | 52–60 |
| 30–39 | 39–44 | 47–54 |
| 40–49 | 35–40 | 43–49 |
| 50–59 | 31–36 | 38–43 |
| 60–69 | 26–31 | 33–38 |

**Trainability:** VO2 max is moderately heritable (~50%), but training can improve it by 15–25% in untrained individuals. Trained athletes see smaller increments. VO2 max peaks typically in the mid-to-late 20s and declines ~10% per decade without training; regular aerobic exercise substantially attenuates this decline.

Garmin estimates VO2 max from sub-maximal heart rate data during outdoor runs. These estimates are useful for tracking trends but may be ±10–15% of laboratory values.

## Training Zones

Training zones are defined relative to maximum heart rate (HRmax, estimated as 220 − age for most purposes):

- **Zone 1 (50–60% HRmax):** Active recovery; promotes blood flow and accelerates metabolic waste clearance. Used on rest/easy days.
- **Zone 2 (60–70% HRmax):** Aerobic base development. The "conversational" zone. Trains fat oxidation, mitochondrial density, and cardiac output. Essential for endurance capacity. Requires long durations (30+ min per session) to be effective.
- **Zone 3 (70–80% HRmax):** Aerobic threshold; moderate intensity. Improves lactate threshold but is metabolically costly without being as potent as Zone 4–5 for VO2 max.
- **Zone 4 (80–90% HRmax):** Threshold training. Improves lactate clearing capacity and "comfortably hard" pace sustainability.
- **Zone 5 (90–100% HRmax):** VO2 max intervals. Short, intense efforts (3–8 min) drive maximal adaptations in cardiac stroke volume and oxygen extraction. High physiological cost; requires full recovery between sessions.

## Resting Heart Rate Trends

Resting heart rate (RHR) decreases as cardiovascular fitness improves — a reflection of increased stroke volume (the heart pumps more blood per beat, needing fewer beats per minute). Reference values:

- **Untrained adults:** 70–90 bpm
- **Fit adults:** 50–70 bpm
- **Trained endurance athletes:** 35–55 bpm (clinical bradycardia, which is benign in this context)

Trending RHR over weeks provides insight into recovery and fitness: rising RHR against a baseline of training consistency signals accumulated fatigue, illness, or poor sleep. Falling RHR over months reflects aerobic adaptation.

## The 80/20 Rule: Polarised Training

Norwegian sports scientist Stephen Seiler's research on elite endurance athletes revealed that most successful training programmes follow a polarised distribution: approximately **80% of sessions at low intensity (Zones 1–2) and 20% at high intensity (Zones 4–5)**, with minimal time in the "moderate" Zone 3.

This model outperforms threshold-dominated or moderate-intensity approaches because:

1. Low-intensity work builds aerobic base without accumulating significant fatigue.
2. High-intensity efforts provide potent stimuli for VO2 max and neuromuscular adaptations.
3. Zone 3 is physiologically costly but provides less specific benefit than either extreme.

For recreational athletes training 5–8 hours per week, strict 80/20 is approximated. The principle is directional: avoid defaulting to "medium hard" for every session.

## Cardiovascular Adaptation Timeline

Meaningful aerobic adaptations require consistent stimulus over weeks to months:

- **2–4 weeks:** Plasma volume expansion (first adaptation; reduces RHR and improves cardiac output at submaximal intensities).
- **4–8 weeks:** Mitochondrial biogenesis and capillary density increases in working muscles; VO2 max begins measurable improvement.
- **8–12 weeks:** Structural cardiac adaptations (eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy in endurance training); sustained VO2 max gains.
- **6–12 months:** Full expression of aerobic base if training is progressive and consistent.

Expectations for VO2 max improvement should be calibrated to this timeline — week-to-week fluctuations in Garmin estimates reflect noise rather than real fitness change.

## Running Efficiency: Pace Per HR and Aerobic Decoupling

**Aerobic decoupling** (also called cardiac drift) is the percentage drift between pace-to-HR ratio in the first and second halves of a run. Low decoupling (<5%) indicates good aerobic fitness and heat adaptation; high decoupling (>10%) suggests the effort was above the aerobic threshold or conditions were stressful.

Tracking pace/HR over time reveals fitness trends: for the same pace, HR gradually decreasing over weeks indicates improving aerobic efficiency — a practical VO2 max proxy.

## SpO2 (Blood Oxygen Saturation)

During sleep, healthy adults maintain SpO2 of 94–100%. Key thresholds:

- **95–100%:** Normal; no concern.
- **90–94%:** Mildly reduced; possible brief desaturation events during deep sleep, common and often benign.
- **<90% (sustained):** Clinically significant; warrants medical evaluation. May indicate sleep apnoea or other respiratory dysfunction. Repeated or prolonged values below 90% should prompt referral.

Garmin's overnight SpO2 monitoring is a screening tool, not a diagnostic one, but persistent low values are a legitimate flag.

## Concurrent Training: Strength and Cardio Interference

The "interference effect" (Hickson, 1980; reviewed by Wilson et al., 2012) describes how simultaneous strength and endurance training in the same programme can blunt maximal adaptation in each domain compared to training only one. The effect is real but context-dependent:

- **Largest interference:** Same-session cardio before strength (fatigues muscles for resistance work). Avoid placing long Zone 4–5 cardio immediately before heavy lifting.
- **Minimal interference:** Separate sessions by 6+ hours; low-intensity Zone 1–2 cardio has negligible interference with strength.
- **Concurrent training is appropriate** for most recreational athletes — the health benefits of both modalities outweigh the modest interference reduction in peak adaptation. Prioritise the primary goal in session ordering.
