# HRV Interpretation: Autonomic Nervous System and Training Readiness

## What HRV Measures

Heart Rate Variability (HRV) quantifies the variation in time intervals between consecutive heartbeats (R-R intervals). Contrary to intuition, a perfectly regular heartbeat is not healthy — it indicates a rigid, unresponsive autonomic nervous system. Healthy hearts continuously modulate beat timing in response to respiration, blood pressure regulation, and neural input.

HRV reflects the balance between the two branches of the autonomic nervous system (ANS):

- **Sympathetic ("fight or flight"):** Dominates during stress, exercise, illness, and under-recovery. Shortens R-R intervals and reduces HRV.
- **Parasympathetic ("rest and digest"):** Dominates during recovery, relaxation, and sleep. Lengthens R-R intervals and increases HRV.

Higher HRV generally indicates greater parasympathetic tone and better capacity to tolerate and recover from physiological stress. Low HRV signals sympathetic dominance — the body is managing a stressor (training, illness, psychological stress, poor sleep).

## The Primary Metric: RMSSD

The most common HRV metric for health and fitness wearables is **RMSSD** (Root Mean Square of Successive Differences), a measure of beat-to-beat variability that is robust to noise and closely tracks parasympathetic activity. Garmin, Polar, and WHOOP all use RMSSD or its natural log (lnRMSSD) as their core HRV metric.

## Normal Ranges and Individual Variation

RMSSD values vary enormously between individuals — from below 20 ms in sedentary older adults to above 100 ms in elite endurance athletes. Age, sex, fitness level, and genetics all influence baseline HRV. Reference population data:

- Adults 20–40 years: median RMSSD approximately 40–60 ms (highly variable)
- Adults 40–60 years: median RMSSD approximately 25–45 ms
- Trained athletes: frequently 60–100+ ms

**Critically, absolute values are less important than individual trends.** An RMSSD of 35 ms might be normal for one person and indicate significant suppression for another. Always interpret HRV relative to an individual's established baseline.

## Garmin's Overnight HRV Measurement

Garmin devices measure HRV during sleep (overnight RMSSD), averaging readings across the night and providing a 5-minute window in the early morning as a standardised measurement. This approach is superior to spot checks because:

1. Sleep minimises confounding variables (posture, recent exercise, food, stress)
2. Overnight averaging smooths night-to-night noise
3. The measurement captures the deepest recovery phase

Garmin presents a 7-day average HRV status baseline and daily deviation. The app colour-codes status (balanced/unbalanced) based on deviation from the rolling average.

## HRV and Training Readiness

HRV is most actionable as a readiness signal when interpreted as a trend over 5–14 days:

- **Rising or stable HRV:** Parasympathetic recovery is adequate; system is adapting to training load. Proceed with planned training.
- **Gradually declining HRV over 3–5 days:** Accumulated fatigue or stress; consider reducing volume or intensity on the next session.
- **Acute single-day dip:** Common; can reflect poor sleep, alcohol, late meal, or transient stress. One low reading is not actionable.
- **Sustained decline (5+ days below baseline):** Signals under-recovery. Reduce training load materially or take a rest day.

## Practical Thresholds

Based on consensus in applied sport science (Kiviniemi, Hautala, Kinnunen & Tulppo framework; HRV4Training guidelines):

- **Within ±10% of 7-day baseline:** Normal variation. Train as planned.
- **10–20% below 7-day baseline:** Caution. Consider reducing volume or intensity. Monitor the next 2 days.
- **>20% below 7-day baseline:** High sympathetic load. Strong recommendation to rest or perform only low-intensity active recovery. Investigate cause (sleep, stress, illness).
- **Consecutive days at 20%+ below baseline:** Indicates functional overreaching. Rest is mandatory.

## HRV Suppression Causes

Understanding why HRV is low is as important as the reading itself:

- **High training load:** Expected post-session suppression (lasts 12–36 hours typically); prolonged suppression indicates insufficient recovery.
- **Poor sleep:** Both sleep deprivation and fragmented sleep acutely reduce HRV.
- **Alcohol:** Even one standard drink suppresses HRV for 12–24 hours. Two or more drinks cause significant overnight HRV reduction.
- **Illness:** HRV drops 1–2 days before overt symptoms appear, making it an early illness warning.
- **Psychological stress:** Work or life stress elevates sympathetic tone; HRV responds to psychological load independent of physical training.
- **Overheating / dehydration:** Both increase sympathetic activity and reduce HRV.

## HRV and Periodisation

HRV is expected to follow the structure of a training block:

- **During accumulation phase:** Gradual HRV decline as fatigue accumulates. This is normal and planned.
- **During intensification phase:** Further HRV suppression; monitor closely to avoid tipping into overreaching.
- **During deload / taper:** HRV rebounds, often rising above pre-block baseline (supercompensation of autonomic function). This is the desired outcome.

## HRV and Resting Heart Rate (Inverse Correlation)

HRV and resting heart rate (RHR) are inversely correlated — as HRV declines, RHR typically rises (and vice versa). Both reflect ANS balance. Tracking both simultaneously provides a more robust readiness signal. When HRV is low *and* RHR is elevated, the combined signal strongly supports rest or reduced training. When they diverge (HRV low but RHR normal), the signal is ambiguous — contextual factors (sleep quality, training log) should guide the decision.
