# Recovery: Physiology, Metrics, and Practical Strategies

## The Recovery Spectrum

Recovery operates across multiple timescales, and distinguishing them is essential for appropriate intervention:

- **Acute recovery (minutes to hours):** Clearance of metabolic by-products (lactate, inorganic phosphate), re-phosphorylation of ATP-PCr stores, cardiovascular return to baseline. Completed within hours of a session.
- **Short-term recovery (24–72 hours):** Muscle protein synthesis, glycogen replenishment, inflammatory resolution, neural recovery from high-intensity work. This is the window most relevant to training frequency decisions.
- **Long-term recovery (weeks to months):** Tissue remodelling, connective tissue adaptation, hormonal baseline restoration after a hard training block. Required after sustained high-volume or high-intensity phases (e.g., competition seasons).

Misidentifying the recovery timescale leads to poor decisions — training through short-term fatigue is productive; training through long-term systemic fatigue accumulates debt.

## Overtraining Syndrome vs Functional Overreaching

These exist on a continuum and require careful differentiation:

- **Functional Overreaching (FOR):** Short-term performance decline following a high-load block; resolves with 1–2 weeks of reduced training. The intended outcome of a hard accumulation phase. HRV and resting HR normalise quickly.
- **Non-Functional Overreaching (NFOR):** More pronounced decline; takes weeks to months to resolve. Performance, mood, and hormonal markers are significantly affected. HRV may take 2–4 weeks to recover.
- **Overtraining Syndrome (OTS):** Full overtraining, lasting months. Characterised by sustained performance decrements, hormonal dysregulation (reduced testosterone, elevated cortisol), psychological symptoms (depression, loss of motivation), and immune suppression. Requires medical support and extended rest.

OTS is rare; NFOR is common among dedicated athletes who ignore early warning signs. The critical differentiator is duration of symptoms and response to rest.

## Body Battery and Readiness Metrics

Garmin's **Body Battery** is a proprietary algorithm estimating energy reserve on a 0–100 scale. It integrates:
- HRV during sleep (primary input)
- Sleep duration and quality
- Stress levels throughout the day
- Activity and training load history

**Training Readiness** (a separate Garmin metric) similarly integrates HRV status, sleep quality, sleep duration, recent load, and recovery time.

Both are composite scores — imperfect but directionally useful. Their value lies in tracking trends over days, not treating single-day values as precise.

**Practical action thresholds for readiness/body battery:**

| Score Range | Interpretation | Recommended Action |
|-------------|---------------|-------------------|
| 75–100 | Well recovered; high readiness | Train hard; push intensity or volume |
| 50–75 | Moderate recovery | Normal training; avoid additional PRs or maximal efforts |
| 25–50 | Partial recovery | Reduce intensity; moderate volume; prioritise technique |
| 0–25 | Low recovery | Full rest or Zone 1 active recovery only |

## Stress, Cortisol, and Adaptation

Cortisol is an essential acute stress hormone — it mobilises energy during exercise and is necessary for adaptation. The problem is **chronic elevation**:

- Sustained cortisol suppresses testosterone production (reduces LH and GnRH signalling)
- Impairs muscle protein synthesis directly by upregulating protein catabolism
- Blunts immune function, increasing upper respiratory illness incidence
- Interferes with sleep quality, creating a negative feedback loop

Garmin's stress score (0–100) uses HRV variability throughout the day to estimate sympathetic activation. Chronic daily stress scores above 60 in the presence of training indicate the body is managing total allostatic load above recovery capacity.

## Practical Recovery Tools: Evidence Ranking

1. **Sleep (highest evidence):** The single most potent recovery intervention. No other strategy compensates for inadequate sleep. 7–9 hours for most adults; potentially 8–10 hours during hard training blocks.
2. **Nutrition — protein timing:** Post-exercise protein ingestion (0.4 g/kg body weight within 2 hours) maximises muscle protein synthesis. A 80 kg athlete targets ~32 g protein post-workout. Total daily protein of 1.6–2.2 g/kg is the most important nutritional variable for muscle recovery.
3. **Nutrition — carbohydrate restoration:** Glycogen is the primary fuel for moderate-to-high intensity exercise. Post-workout carbohydrate (1–1.2 g/kg in the first hour) restores glycogen at the fastest rate, most relevant when training sessions are <8 hours apart.
4. **Active recovery:** Low-intensity Zone 1 exercise (walking, easy cycling) accelerates blood lactate clearance and reduces perceived muscle soreness without adding adaptive stress. Superior to complete inactivity for short-term recovery.
5. **Cold water immersion (CWI):** Reduces muscle soreness and inflammation acutely. Evidence suggests CWI may blunt hypertrophic signalling if applied immediately post-strength training — best reserved for competition recovery, not routine training.
6. **Sauna (heat therapy):** Post-exercise sauna increases growth hormone and plasma volume; some evidence for improved cardiovascular efficiency. Dehydration risk must be managed.
7. **Compression, massage, foam rolling:** Reduce perceived soreness with moderate evidence; minimal effect on actual performance recovery. Useful for comfort.

## Overtraining Warning Signs in Wearable Data

Recognising early overtraining signals allows proactive load management before a significant performance or health setback:

- **Sustained HRV decline (5+ consecutive days below baseline):** The most reliable early indicator. Particularly concerning when unrelated to a specific acute cause (illness, alcohol).
- **Elevated resting heart rate (>5 bpm above baseline for 3+ days):** Sympathetic dominance persisting into rest periods; mirror signal to HRV decline.
- **Declining performance despite consistent training:** When progressive overload stalls or reverses despite adequate training stimulus — adaptation is failing.
- **Persistent low Body Battery (maxes out below 50 for multiple days):** The composite score captures cumulative fatigue across sleep, HRV, and stress simultaneously.
- **Declining sleep quality despite adequate opportunity:** Impaired sleep despite getting to bed on time often reflects hormonal disruption (elevated cortisol interfering with melatonin and sleep architecture).
- **Rising training load with no planned deload:** Programmes without built-in recovery weeks progressively accumulate fatigue; if MRV is being approached without a deload week scheduled, risk of NFOR rises significantly.

When two or more of these signals appear simultaneously, the appropriate response is always to reduce load or rest — the cost of unnecessary rest is low; the cost of progressing into overtraining syndrome is high.
